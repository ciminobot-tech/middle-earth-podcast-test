# Episode 1 — Before Sauron

**Narrator:** Isabella (`bf_isabella`)  
**Core running time:** approximately 22 minutes at a calm 145 words per minute  
**Optional listener paths:** 2–3 minutes each  
**Tone:** intimate, clear, atmospheric, and grounded. Never assume the listener knows the books.

## Opening — 0:00–2:10

Before there was a Ring. Before there was a hobbit. Before there was even a place called Mordor in the story we know — there was a world that had already been wounded.

Welcome to Middle-earth.

This is not a story that begins with a king, a castle, or a map. It begins with a question that keeps returning through every age: what happens when someone stops wanting to live in the world, and starts wanting to own it?

That question begins with Morgoth. Sauron comes later.

If you know *The Lord of the Rings*, you already know the final shape of the danger. A dark tower. A watching eye. Armies moving out of Mordor. A Ring that seems far too small to carry so much fear.

But the Ring is not the beginning. It is a surviving piece of a much older pattern.

For this first journey, you only need three names.

Eru Ilúvatar is Tolkien’s creator: the source of the world and of the beings who first enter it.

The Valar are powerful spirits who come into the world to shape it and care for it. They are not gods in the simple sense. They are more like guardians and makers inside a creation that is larger than any of them.

And Morgoth — first called Melkor — is the greatest rebel among them.

Sauron will become the enemy most people remember. Morgoth is the older shadow behind him.

There is something useful about starting here. It stops the story becoming a pile of strange names. The history of Middle-earth is not really about memorising names. It is about following pressure. Beauty is made. Someone wants to possess it. Trust is broken. A small choice carries further than anyone expects.

That is the shape of this world. And it is why the Ring matters.

## The world before the map — 2:10–4:45

Tolkien’s early history is told in more than one form, and some details changed as he developed it over decades. We will be honest where that happens. But the broad shape is steady.

Before the physical world exists, there are the Ainur: spiritual beings made by Eru Ilúvatar. Melkor is one of them. He is gifted, powerful, and restless.

His problem is not simply that he enjoys ruin. That would make him too simple.

Melkor wants to create. But he does not want to create within a shared world. He wants his own ideas to be the centre of everything. He wants other things to answer to him.

That distinction matters.

There is a difference between shaping something and possessing it. Between making a home and deciding that everyone inside it must serve your design. Tolkien keeps returning to that difference.

When the world — Arda — is made, the Valar enter it to give it form. They raise land, shape seas, bring order where they can. Melkor enters too.

And he disrupts what they make.

He does not merely attack with armies. At first, the conflict is built into the world itself. Light is broken. Land is damaged. Things meant to last are made unstable.

That is why Middle-earth feels ancient even before its famous stories begin. By the time Frodo is born, the world has already been through catastrophes so large that whole countries have disappeared.

The Shire is quiet because it is small and sheltered. Not because danger has never existed.

Rivendell is beautiful because it has endured. Not because it is untouched.

And the old songs that people sing in *The Lord of the Rings* are not background decoration. They are records of losses so old that most people no longer understand them fully.

Morgoth becomes the first great enemy because he turns his gifts toward control. Sauron, much later, learns from him.

**Listener stop:** We can keep this simple, go deeper on the creation story, or carry on to the first great losses.

## The light that was lost — 4:45–8:05

In the early ages, the western land of Aman is lit by two great Trees: one silver, one gold.

This is not sunlight and moonlight as we know them. It is an older light. A light that becomes a memory inside the world.

The Elves have awakened far away in Middle-earth. Some begin a long journey west. Among them are the Noldor: Elves known for learning, craft, language, curiosity, and skill.

Their greatest maker is Fëanor.

Fëanor creates three jewels called the Silmarils. They hold the light of the Two Trees. They are objects of extraordinary beauty, but they are not just treasure. They become bound up with pride, grief, love, and the fear of losing what cannot be replaced.

Meanwhile, Melkor is captured for a time. It would be easy to think that this ends the danger.

It does not.

He is released under watch. He speaks carefully. He feeds distrust. He does not need everyone to believe him. He only needs people to start doubting one another.

Then he escapes, joined by Ungoliant, a being of hunger and darkness. Together they destroy the Two Trees.

The act changes everything.

The world loses its first great light. Fëanor’s father, Finwë, is killed. The Silmarils are stolen. And grief becomes an oath.

Fëanor and his sons swear that they will recover the jewels from anyone who holds them.

The oath is one of the great disasters of the First Age. It does not make them simply evil. It makes them trapped by a promise made in anger and pain — a promise they keep trying to honour even when honour has already left it.

This is one of Tolkien’s clearest warnings: a beautiful goal can become destructive when possession matters more than people.

The Noldor leave the west and return to Middle-earth. They go to make war on Morgoth.

Their courage is real. Their suffering is real. But the damage they carry with them is real too.

The First Age is not a clean story of heroes marching toward victory. It is an age where pride, loyalty, love, rage, and endurance are all mixed together.

That is why its stories still matter thousands of years later.

## The First Age: a world of ruins — 8:05–11:55

For a while, the Elves build kingdoms in the north-west of Middle-earth, in a region later called Beleriand.

There is Doriath, deep in a protected forest. Nargothrond, hidden by a river. Gondolin, a secret city in the mountains. There are harbours, woodland realms, fortresses, and houses held together by loyalty.

But Morgoth has his own stronghold in the north: Angband.

From there, he sends out armies, wolves, dragons, and servants. The geography matters because the war is not abstract. People build places of refuge, and then live with the fear that refuge can fail.

Three stories from this age become especially important later.

The first is Beren and Lúthien.

Beren is a mortal man. Lúthien is an Elf, and the daughter of a king. Their love crosses a boundary that most people believe cannot be crossed. To win the right to marry her, Beren is sent on what should be an impossible task: to take a Silmaril from Morgoth’s crown.

The story is dangerous, strange, and deeply personal. It says that courage is not the same as strength. It can be a person continuing after every sensible reason to stop has disappeared.

The second is the story of Túrin.

Túrin is brave, gifted, and surrounded by damage. His life is shaped by pride, bad information, grief, and a curse that follows his family. It is one of Tolkien’s bleakest stories, and it matters because it shows how a person can be strong without being safe from tragedy.

The third is Gondolin.

Gondolin is hidden for years. It becomes a place of beauty, craft, and hope. But hidden does not mean permanent. When it falls, survivors escape with a child named Eärendil.

Eärendil will matter because he carries a plea for help to the west.

By the end of the First Age, the war against Morgoth becomes too large for the peoples of Middle-earth to win alone. The Valar finally intervene. Morgoth is defeated and cast out of the world.

But the victory is not tidy.

Beleriand — the land where so many of these stories happened — is broken and mostly drowned beneath the sea.

This is the first major lesson of the history: even when evil is defeated, the world does not return to exactly what it was before.

Loss leaves a map behind.

## What Sauron learned — 11:55–14:45

Sauron begins as a servant of Morgoth.

Like Morgoth, he is a Maia: one of the lesser Ainur. Lesser here does not mean unimportant. Gandalf and Saruman are also Maiar. It means that they are beneath the Valar in rank, while still far beyond ordinary people.

Sauron’s strength is not the same as Morgoth’s.

Morgoth is vast, violent, and possessive. He corrupts things on a huge scale.

Sauron is more orderly.

He likes systems. He likes planning. He likes structures where everyone has a place — as long as he decides the place.

That makes him frightening in a different way.

After Morgoth’s defeat, Sauron is not destroyed. For a time, he hides. The surviving peoples believe the great war is over. And in one sense, it is.

But the wish to control does not disappear just because its first master has fallen.

Sauron returns in the Second Age. At first, he does not present himself as a dark lord surrounded by smoke and soldiers. He comes in a fair and helpful form.

This is important.

He does not begin by demanding obedience. He begins by offering knowledge.

In Eregion, an Elven realm famous for craft, smiths are making beautiful and powerful things. Sauron teaches them new techniques. He presents himself as someone who can help them preserve beauty against time and loss.

That promise has weight. The Elves know that the world changes. They know kingdoms fade. They know the light of earlier ages is already gone.

Sauron understands this fear. And he offers an answer that sounds almost generous.

Why let beautiful things pass away, he implies, when power can help you keep them?

That is the doorway to the Rings of Power.

## The Rings of Power — 14:45–18:25

The Rings are not all made in the same way, and they are not all used by the same people.

The Elven smiths of Eregion make Rings of Power, including the Three Rings later kept by the Elves. Sauron secretly makes the One Ring alone, in Mordor.

The One Ring is not simply a very dangerous piece of jewellery. It contains much of Sauron’s own power.

Its purpose is domination.

It is made to influence the other Rings and to bring their wearers under Sauron’s control.

The Elves sense the danger quickly. They remove their Three Rings before Sauron can use the One to master them.

But the larger damage is already done.

Other Rings go to Dwarf-lords and to Men.

The Dwarves are not turned into Sauron’s servants in the same way. Their resistance and their own nature make that part of his plan fail. But the Rings deepen their hunger for wealth and help create new dangers around treasure and power.

Nine Men receive Rings. Over time, they become the Nazgûl: the Ringwraiths.

They are not ghosts in the simple sense. They are people whose lives have been stretched beyond a natural end, until their own wills are almost entirely swallowed by Sauron’s power.

This is what the Ring offers. Not a sudden explosion of evil. A gradual trade.

You are given something that looks like strength. You use it. You start to rely on it. And slowly, the thing that promised to help you decides what you are for.

That is why wise people fear the Ring even when they believe they could use it well.

It does not only give power. It teaches a way of thinking.

Possess. Command. Make people into tools.

And that is Morgoth’s old lesson, still alive in a smaller object.

## The fall of Númenor and the Last Alliance — 18:25–21:35

Sauron’s next great victory does not begin with a battle either.

It begins with Númenor.

Númenor is an island kingdom of Men, raised up after the First Age. Its people are gifted with long life, skill, and a great seafaring culture. Over time, though, many of them become afraid of death.

Sauron is taken there as a prisoner. But he turns the situation around. He gains influence over the king. He feeds the fear that the Númenóreans are being denied something they deserve: immortality.

The result is catastrophe.

The king attempts to seize the Undying Lands by force. Númenor is destroyed. The world itself is changed.

Some survivors escape, led by Elendil and his sons. They establish kingdoms in Middle-earth: Arnor in the north and Gondor in the south.

Sauron also survives, though he loses the fair appearance he once used to deceive people.

War follows.

Elves and Men form the Last Alliance. They march against Sauron in Mordor. After long fighting, Sauron is overthrown. Isildur cuts the One Ring from his hand.

This is the moment when the Ring could have been destroyed.

It is not.

Isildur keeps it.

It is easy to judge him from a distance. But this is one more reason the Ring works as a story. It does not tempt only foolish people. It tempts people who have survived terrible things and believe, for a moment, that they deserve to hold on to a prize.

The Ring is lost in a river. Centuries pass.

It is found by Déagol. Taken by Sméagol. Kept in darkness for hundreds of years. Then found by Bilbo Baggins.

And eventually, it reaches Frodo.

## Why the smallest person carries it — 21:35–23:20

By the time Frodo inherits the Ring, Middle-earth is not one united world.

Gondor is still powerful, but tired. Rohan is its ally. The Shire is small and protected. Rivendell and Lórien hold on to older beauty that is slowly fading. Dwarves have returned to the Lonely Mountain. Sauron is gathering strength in Mordor.

No one can simply use the Ring safely.

Gandalf will not take it. Galadriel will not take it. Aragorn will not take it.

They understand what it would ask of them.

Frodo is not chosen because he is the strongest person in Middle-earth. He is chosen because this task cannot be solved by strength alone.

The Ring must be carried into the land where it was made and destroyed there.

That is the strange, stubborn heart of the story.

The answer to domination is not better domination.

The answer is refusing it.

So when the story of *The Lord of the Rings* begins, it is already standing on thousands of years of memory: lost light, drowned lands, broken oaths, hidden cities, rings of power, and people who keep learning too late what control costs.

The Ring is small because the danger is not only in the object.

It is in the desire to make the world obey.

Next, we can follow the Ring from Isildur to Frodo. We can go back to the First Age and meet Beren, Lúthien, Túrin, and the people behind the old songs. Or we can map the powers at war when the Fellowship begins.

For now, remember this: Sauron is ancient. But he is not the beginning. Morgoth came first. And the first shadow began with the wish to own the world instead of sharing it.

## Optional listener paths

### Keep it simple: Morgoth, Sauron, and the Ring — 2 minutes

Morgoth is the first and larger enemy. Sauron is his servant, who survives and continues the same project in a more organised form. The Ring is the tool Sauron makes to control other people. It matters because it carries part of his own power and his way of thinking.

### Go deeper: Why do versions sometimes differ? — 3 minutes

Tolkien worked on this world for most of his life. *The Lord of the Rings* is a completed published story. *The Silmarillion* was assembled and edited after Tolkien’s death from a large body of writing. *Unfinished Tales* and the *History of Middle-earth* preserve later drafts, alternatives, and developments. The broad spine used in this episode is stable; exact dates and some details can vary. When they matter, we will label that rather than pretending every late draft is one final answer.

### Go deeper: What is a Maia? — 2 minutes

A Maia is one of the Ainur beneath the Valar. “Lesser” is a rank, not a measure of importance. Sauron, Gandalf, Saruman, and Melian are all Maiar. They can shape events on the scale of whole ages, but they are not all alike in purpose or character.
